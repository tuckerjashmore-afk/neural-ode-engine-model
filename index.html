Identifying a grey-box engine model from consumer ECU datalogs

A neural-ODE model of a turbocharged Subaru EJ255 that fit the measured data well and got the physics badly wrong. How that was diagnosed, and the staged identification method that fixed it.

01

The problem

Tuning a turbocharged engine means choosing spark advance, fuel target and boost at every operating point, subject to not destroying the engine. The interesting question is what happens at points you have never run.

That requires a model that extrapolates, which requires the model's internal quantities to mean what they claim to mean. Two standard options both fail that requirement. Black-box regression on logged data interpolates well and extrapolates arbitrarily, because nothing in it is constrained to be physical. A full physical mean-value model extrapolates correctly but needs calibration data a consumer datalog does not contain: compressor and turbine maps for the specific unit, volumetric efficiency surfaces, wall-film parameters.

The grey-box, or Universal Differential Equation, formulation takes the third path. Keep the conservation laws and flow relations as a fixed ODE skeleton, and let neural networks stand in for exactly the constitutive maps that are genuinely unknown. Train by integrating the ODE and back-propagating trajectory error onto the network weights through the solver adjoint.

Target hardware is a Subaru EJ255, 2.5 L flat-four, TD04-13T turbo. The data is stock ECU datalogs at roughly 50 Hz: six logs across three logging platforms, 7,109 rows in the merged set, plus the LiU-ICE academic engine benchmark run through the same pipeline as an external check.

Volume is not the difficulty. Which channels a stock ECU exposes is. A dyno cell measures airflow, exhaust temperature, turbo speed and torque directly. A stock ECU gives manifold pressure, engine speed, throttle position, commanded lambda, spark advance and an inferred engine load. Exactly one of those is a trajectory the model can be fit against. Everything else is an input.

02

The model

Four states are integrated in time: intake manifold pressure, turbo speed, fuel wall-film mass, and metal temperature (frozen here, since its time constant far exceeds the shooting window).

Turbo speed is carried as a sigmoid logit rather than directly. That matters more than it looks: the bound becomes a property of the coordinate, so the state cannot go negative and the Jacobian self-zeros at the rails instead of producing a discontinuous adjoint at a clamp. An earlier version integrated turbo speed directly and clamped it, and the clamp masked the identifiability failure below, because manifold pressure still fit while the underlying state ranged from −927,000 to 57,000 rad/s.

Right-hand side: mean-value engine model

mdot_cyl  = eta_v * P_m * V_d * N / (2 * R * T_m)      speed-density
P_boost   = P_atm * comp_map(omega, mdot_cyl)
mdot_thr  = throttle_flow(alpha, P_boost, T_m, P_m)    compressible orifice
P_exh     = P_atm + K * mdot_exh^2
domega    = 1000*(P_turbine - P_compressor)/(I_t*omega) - C_friction*omega/I_t

dP_m/dt      = (R*T_m/V_m) * (mdot_thr - mdot_cyl)
dm_puddle/dt = X*mdot_f_inj - m_puddle/tau

Eleven neural maps replace the constitutive relations. Each is a 2×16 dense-tanh chain with an output activation enforcing its physical range: volumetric efficiency to [0, 1.2], compressor and turbine efficiency to [0.4, 0.85], pressure ratio to [1, 5]. Inputs are normalised by a fixed per-map scale, because raw engine speed near 6,000 and turbo speed near 100,000 saturate tanh into dead gradients on the first forward pass.

Training is multiple shooting on ~1 s windows, Tsit5 for integration, InterpolatingAdjoint with a ReverseDiffVJP for gradients, and Adam over a 100-epoch MSE warm-start followed by a 300-epoch Gaussian NLL fine-tune across mean and variance heads. Six ensemble members train concurrently and supply the predictive variance.

03

What went wrong

Joint training converged. Solve rate 100 %, held-out manifold-pressure RMSE 9.8 kPa on a signal spanning about 100 kPa. By the usual metric the model was working. The maps were not.

Fitted internal quantities against physical reality
QuantityFittedPhysical
Volumetric efficiency at WOT1.06~0.77
Turbo speed at full boost~1,300 rad/s11,000–17,000 rad/s
Compressor pressure-ratio curveflatpeaks near 1.8
Exhaust backpressure112 kPa, below boostmust exceed boost

Volumetric efficiency above 1.0 means more air entering the cylinder than the cylinder displaces. Turbo speed of 1,300 rad/s at full boost is the compressor barely turning while producing 1.5 bar. Exhaust pressure below boost pressure means the turbine is being driven backwards.

These errors cancel. Low turbo speed gives low pressure ratio; inflated volumetric efficiency compensates by pulling more air through the same manifold pressure; the trajectory fits.

Eleven unknown maps trained against one observable trajectory is structurally degenerate. Loss reweighting cannot fix it, because reweighting changes which combination the optimiser lands on, not the dimension of the solution manifold.

Ten full training runs confirmed that empirically before the structural reading was accepted. A volumetric-efficiency label anchor, a boost-channel weight, a boost-weighted per-timestep loss, a hard airflow constraint, a throttle recalibration and a frozen compressor-map lookup were each tried. Each moved the compensation somewhere else. Two made the model worse.

04

Finding the actual causes

Each cause was isolated with a cheap targeted probe rather than another training run.

  1. Turbo speed collapses because the turbine is energy-negative. Decomposing domega at a high-boost point showed the turbine producing ~0.6 kW against a ~1.5 kW compressor load. With that sign the state must decelerate, and no penalty term can hold it up. It was confirmed dead up to a weight of 5,000, by which point the penalty had wrecked the pressure fit and the turbo speed had barely moved.
  2. The turbine is starved because backpressure is tiny. Measured 112 kPa against 139 kPa of boost, which is physically backwards. Since the backpressure coefficient was plausible, airflow was wrong.
  3. Airflow is wrong because volumetric efficiency underfits. It sat at 0.38 against a 0.76 label. It was matched only in the NLL output loss, where channels sum equally, so a residual of order 0.1² is drowned by torque residuals in N·m² and EGT residuals in K².

That closes the loop. Backpressure scales with the square of airflow, so a volumetric efficiency at half its true value gives a quarter of the backpressure, which starves the turbine, drains the turbo speed, kills boost, lowers manifold pressure and lowers airflow again. The system is bistable and the shooting solver falls into the collapsed basin.

A separate high-boost shortfall localised to the throttle. Subaru logs about 0.44 for “throttle opening angle” at wide-open throttle by drive-by-wire convention, and the effective-area curve evaluated there gave only about 22 % of full area. The model was choking an engine its own compressor map was correctly boosting.

One more probe corrected an assumption carried for weeks: the logs are not quasi-steady. The wide-open-throttle log contains 182 spool events. Turbo speed was not unidentifiable for lack of transients. It was unidentifiable because a free neural compressor map keyed on airflow alone gives the turbo speed state no gradient path at all.

05

Staged identification

The fix follows from the diagnosis. If the joint problem is degenerate, do not solve the joint problem. Identify each map against its own ground truth, offline, with no ODE in the loop, in an order where each stage leaves exactly one unknown.

The staged pipeline
StepMapGround truthResult
1eta_vlogged engine load (g/rev)RMSE 0.033
2comp_mapreal boost, by air-path inversionRMSE 0.012 PR
3eta_tturbo speed from power balanceRMSE 0.017
4egtphysics exhaust-temp pseudo-labelRMSE 4.3 K
—eta_cdigitized efficiency islandsfrozen lookup

Step 2 is the one worth explaining

With volumetric efficiency and throttle pinned, the steady manifold balance throttle_flow(…) = cylinder_flow(…) has exactly one unknown, boost pressure, and is invertible at every logged point. That recovers the compressor's actual operating points: the boost this specific turbo made, not the boost the model wanted it to make. Reading turbo speed off the digitized manufacturer map at those points gives (omega, mdot, PR) triples, and the compressor map becomes a plain supervised regression: the sole remaining unknown, no ODE, no degeneracy. The recovered map peaks near PR 1.8, differing from the generic digitized map that had been under-producing.

The inversion is only valid where the manifold is at steady state. Vacuum and deceleration points have throttle flow below cylinder flow, so the fit uses boost points above PR 1.1 only.

Step 3 uses the same trick one level up

With the air path frozen, inverting the compressor map at the measured pressure ratio gives a turbo speed target for every logged point. Steady-state shaft balance then solves for the eta_t × T_exh product, and turbine efficiency is fit to that. The product is a genuine limitation rather than a shortcut: this log set has no EGT sensor and the two factors multiply, so only their product is identifiable. It happens to be the product the turbo speed ODE depends on, so the model is correct where it is used, but neither factor is separately calibrated.

Results

The turbo speed state now tracks the physics-derived target at correlation 0.948 with a median ratio of 1.013, within 1.3 % across the operating range, against a flat collapse before.

Where the residual lives

An inconsistency probe compared the compressor map fed the ODE's turbo speed against the same map fed the inverted target.

Compressor map consistency probe

measP  predP | om_state  om_targ | PR_meas  PR@state  PR@target
150.5  117.1 |    6416     9538  |  1.486     1.196      1.491
159.9  123.1 |    7281    10318  |  1.578     1.252      1.588

PR@target matches the measured pressure ratio to three digits and so the compressor map is correct. The ODE's turbo speed is 30 % below target so the turbo dynamics were wrong. The correct map, fed a wrong speed, correctly produces a wrong boost. Turbine efficiency had been left free in that retrain and had become the new compensating degree of freedom, which is what motivated staging it. Accurate maps are not an accurate model when one link in the chain is broken.

The ~15 kPa figure is a measured ceiling, not an excuse. Two independent attempts to lower it (soft anchoring instead of hard freezing, and pretraining the combustion maps) shifted the residual profile while leaving the overall number unchanged. Both refinements are sound, and both confirmed the ceiling rather than raising it.

07

What the exercise established

  1. The turbo speed collapse was a symptom of a wrong volumetric efficiency, not a turbo-physics problem or a data problem. Pinning volumetric efficiency to measured airflow fixed the turbo, and the penalty term built to fix it directly turned out to be a no-op, with identical results at weights of 0, 1 and 100.
  2. Most “learned” maps were observable or already known. Only torque, EGT, knock and wall-film are genuinely unobserved on this data and should be learned at all.
  3. A better loss number from a grey-box model is not evidence of a better model. Check every internal quantity against its physical range before believing a residual.
  4. Single-member smoke tests are a different training regime. Two loss-weight experiments looked like clear wins at one member and 40 epochs and reversed at full training, including one where the under-trained run had the opposite sign of bias to the converged run.
08

Limits, and what would move them

The dataset is six logs across three logging platforms (Cobb, RomRaider and Viceman) totalling 7,109 rows in the merged set, alongside the LiU-ICE academic engine benchmark converted into the same pipeline as an external check on the cleaning and windowing code. Volume is not the constraint. Two specific missing measurements are.

No exhaust-gas temperature channel. Turbine efficiency and exhaust temperature multiply in the turbine power balance, so only their product is identifiable. That product is what the turbo speed ODE depends on, so the model is correct where it is used, but neither factor is separately calibrated.

No wastegate-duty sweep. Stepping wastegate duty across a single pull is what observes turbine efficiency against boost independently of the rest of the air path.

Those two set the ~15 kPa floor, and both are an afternoon of instrumented driving. Knowing precisely which two measurements would move the result is the useful output of the whole exercise.

Two further caveats worth stating plainly. Cross-log generalisation was measured at 45.3 kPa, but at an earlier model predating staged identification, so that figure is stale rather than current. And the uncertainty calibration uses a vehicle-dynamics torque pseudo-label rather than a dyno; the fitted scale factor of 5.29 says the raw ensemble is roughly five times overconfident against that label.

Implementation. Julia with SciML (OrdinaryDiffEq, SciMLSensitivity, Lux, Zygote) for identification and training; pure-Python inference from an exported JSON artifact with no Julia at runtime. Both sides implement the same equations independently and are held to bit-for-bit agreement by a cross-language parity test. That is how two silent bugs were caught, unnormalised network inputs and a column-major weight reshape.
